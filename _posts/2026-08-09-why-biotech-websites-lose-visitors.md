---
title: Why biotech websites lose visitors in the first 10 seconds

---

Many biotech home pages ask a visitor to decode the company before they can understand why it matters. The first screen leads with a platform name, a broad promise about innovation, and a navigation full of technical categories.

The visitor is left to assemble the story.

That is a costly first impression. An investor, potential partner, prospective employee, or journalist may all arrive with different goals, but each needs the same basic orientation: what kind of problem is this company solving, for whom, and why is its approach worth another minute?

## The first screen has three jobs

A strong opening does not need to explain the entire business. It needs to establish three things quickly:

1. **Field:** Where are we? Diagnostics, therapeutics, devices, research infrastructure?
2. **Change:** What becomes possible because this organisation exists?
3. **Evidence:** What gives the claim substance—a platform, expertise, validation, or a specific advantage?

If the visitor cannot find those signals, even a beautiful website can feel vague.

## “Innovative solutions” is not positioning

Generic language often enters scientific websites because teams are trying to stay accurate before every claim is final. The result is copy that could belong to almost any company: *pioneering, cutting-edge, next-generation, transformative*.

These words describe ambition, not difference.

Specificity is more credible. Name the problem. Indicate the mechanism or operational advantage at the right level. Show what stage the work has reached. A modest, concrete statement usually carries more authority than a grand, ungrounded one.

## Navigation should reduce the work

Your internal structure does not need to become your website structure. A visitor may not know which division, modality, or workstream contains the answer they need.

Organise the route around understandable decisions:

- What the company does.
- How the approach works.
- Where the evidence sits.
- Who is behind it.
- How to start a conversation.

Specialist detail can live beneath that structure. The top-level navigation should make the site easier to enter.

## Build from message hierarchy, not page furniture

Before discussing hero images, animations, or card layouts, write the message hierarchy in plain text. What must the visitor understand first? What question follows naturally? What proof belongs near each claim?

Design can then reinforce that order through scale, spacing, contrast, and interaction. Without a hierarchy, visual polish only makes the confusion more attractive.

The best first screen creates momentum. It gives the visitor enough confidence to keep going—and enough clarity to choose where to go next.
